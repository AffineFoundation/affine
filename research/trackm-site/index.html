GAD arena — king-of-the-hill adversarial distillation

Miners submit small models and try to pass them off as the teacher. A frozen judge reads paired rollouts — one from the miner, one from the teacher — and guesses which is which. Your score is your fool rate: the fraction of turns the judge gets wrong. Beat the reigning king by three points and you take the throne — but every crowning trains a fresh judge, from scratch, on everything the arena has seen. The only model that survives that ratchet is one that actually is the teacher's distribution. Bluffs get learned. Distillation doesn't.

Mechanism

why the only stable throne is real distillation

The cast. A fixed public teacher — Qwen3.8-27B — and a discriminator D: the same 27B base plus a LoRA adapter, trained as an A/B judge on one question only — which of these two rollouts came from the teacher? Miners submit small models (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B MoE, ~3B active params) and are scored by fool rate: the fraction of eval turns where the judge mistakes the miner's rollout for the teacher's. A validity gate runs first — a rollout must parse into (thought, action) with a well-formed, executable action, or the turn is forfeit.

King of the hill. One checkpoint holds the throne. A challenger dethrones it by beating the king's fool rate by at least 3pp over at least 400 paired turns — same judge, same prompts, paired head-to-head. No lucky streaks: the margin and the turn floor make a crown a statistical event, not a coin flip.

The crown event. When a challenger wins, evals pause. A brand-new judge LoRA is trained from scratch on all accumulated eval data — so it specializes in the new king's tells — then published openly. Public D, no lag: every miner sees exactly the judge they must beat. Evals resume, and the judge stays frozen for the whole reign.

Why it can't be gamed. Any surface trick that fools judge D-n becomes training data for judge D-n+1. But a king that truly matches the teacher's distribution gives the fresh judge nothing to learn — its held-out accuracy is pinned at 0.5, a coin flip, no matter how much data it trains on. That is the fixed point of the game, and it's the ratchet metric: each king's fool rate at reign start, against its own fresh judge, across reigns. If that number climbs, checkpoints are genuinely converging on the teacher — not on the judge's blind spots.
