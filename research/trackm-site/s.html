Self-play control — the recursive check

The generator starts as an exact copy of the teacher — zero LoRA, identical distribution by construction. Then the adversarial game runs anyway: an online judge keeps retraining to tell G from the teacher. If the machinery is honest, there is nothing to learn and the game sits at the 0.5 equilibrium forever. If fool rate or judge accuracy drifts away from a coin flip, that motion is an artifact of the pipeline itself — a leak, an optimizer push, a typicality slide — and it calibrates how much of Track M's signal to trust.

Protocol

what a control run is allowed to show

The setup. G is Qwen3.8-27B with a zero-initialized LoRA — bit-for-bit the teacher's distribution (identity check: eff=0.000000). Teacher references are sampled fresh each round on the same server, same params, same template; no cached teacher text is ever scored. The judge D retrains every third round on the latest (G-best, fresh-teacher) pairs. Everything else matches the Track M game.

The leak instrumentation. Every D update measures three held-out accuracies on never-trained turns: held_live (D vs current G), held_ctrl (D vs a frozen zero-adapter G — truth is 0.5 forever), and held_tt (teacher vs teacher — 0.5 by construction). Sustained ctrl/tt above ~0.58 means the pipeline itself leaks a tell (formatting, sampler fingerprint, template skew) — and the same tell would contaminate Track M.

The readout. Fool ≈ 0.5 with flat held-out accuracy = a stable equilibrium — the game is honest. Held-out accuracy climbing while fool falls and typicality rises = drift — the optimizer is pushing G off the teacher manifold even with nothing to learn. Early held-out > 0.6 = leak. Fool held above 0.55 across D updates = G out-competing the judge. The verdict badge above applies exactly this rule to the last five rounds.
